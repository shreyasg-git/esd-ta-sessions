03

OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect

Coreaccess vs identity

Refresher

Authentication answers "who are you?"; authorization answers "what may you do?" — the 401 and the 403 of React Lab 3 · §09. Keep the two apart: the two standards below split along exactly that line.

The problem

Your app wants to know who someone is, without that person creating yet another password — and without your app ever seeing their Google password. Google already knows who they are. The question is how Google can tell your app, in a way your app can trust.

OAuth 2.0 — access, on someone's behalf

OAuth 2.0 is the standard for delegated authorization: "let this app read my Google Calendar", without giving the app your password. The user agrees on Google's page, and the app gets an access token it sends to Google's APIs. An access token means "the bearer may do X with this person's data". It's for calling APIs. It was never designed to tell the app, reliably, who the person is.

OpenID Connect — identity, on top of OAuth

OpenID Connect (OIDC) is a thin layer on top of OAuth 2.0 that adds exactly that: an ID token. It's a statement Google signs: "This is user 104412345678901234567, email asha.rao@example.com — verified — signing in to the app with client ID 1234…, just now; believe this for one hour." It's addressed to your app, and your app reads it.

Access token (OAuth 2.0)ID token (OpenID Connect)
AnswersWhat may the bearer do?Who is this person?
Who reads itGoogle's APIs (Calendar, Drive…)Your app — your backend
FormatWhatever Google likes; opaque to youA JWT: claims you can read, a signature you can check
In this labNot usedThe whole point

"Sign in with Google" is OIDC: we ask Google for identity only — the openid, email and profile scopes, nothing else. Your app never touches anyone's Google data, and never calls a Google API on their behalf.

The words you'll see

  • Identity provider (IdP) — the party that knows who people are. Google, here.
  • Relying party, or client — the app that relies on the IdP's word. Your app.
  • Client ID — the relying party's public name at Google. One per app; everyone in this course shares the TAs' one.
  • Scopes — what the app asks for. openid email profile: who you are, your email, your name and picture.
  • Consent screen — Google's page where the person sees which app is asking.

And one thing that doesn't change: your backend still has its own users and its own tokens. Google answers "who is this?" once, at the door. After that, your app runs exactly as before (section 05).

Q1An app wants to put events into the user's Google Calendar. Is an ID token enough?
Show answer

No. An ID token says who the person is; it doesn't let anyone call Google Calendar. For that the app needs an access token with a calendar scope — OAuth 2.0 proper, with a consent screen that names the scope. This lab needs identity only, so it never asks for one.

Q2Name two things your app gains by letting Google do the sign-in.
Show answer

There's no password for your app to store, hash, reset or leak. And Google's account security — two-factor, suspicious-login checks — protects your sign-in for free. A third: Google has already verified the email address. (The cost: you depend on Google being reachable, and on checking its tokens correctly — sections 05 and 11.)

04

The ID token — a JWT you can read

Coreheader.payload.signature

Refresher

Your API's token is a JWT too, and your React app treats it as opaque: it never decodes it (React Lab 3 · §09). Nothing about that changes. Google's ID token is a different token, from a different issuer, and here we take one apart — to see what your backend has to check.

Three parts, two dots

A JWT is three pieces of base64url text joined by dots: header.payload.signature. base64url is ordinary base64 with - and _ instead of + and / (so it's safe in a URL), and without the = padding. It's an encoding, not encryption: anyone can turn it back. Here is a token shaped exactly like the one Google hands your app:

The course API keys accounts by email, because the contract does (openapi.yaml has no column for sub). A bigger system would store sub too: if someone changes the email on their Google account, sub still says it's them.

The signature: RS256, and Google's public keys

The header says "alg": "RS256": an RSA signature over header.payload, with SHA-256. RSA uses a key pair. Google signs with a private key that never leaves Google. Anyone can check a signature with the matching public key, and Google publishes its public keys as a JWKS (JSON Web Key Set). The header's kid (key id) says which key signed this token; Google rotates its keys every so often, so there are usually two or three.

Do this now — look at Google's keys

Open Google's public keys — the address is www.googleapis.com/oauth2/v3/certs. A keys array: each key has a kid, alg: "RS256", and n and e — the RSA public key. Public, by design: that's the whole trick. Your backend fetches this URL to check every Google token. (Is the sample's kid in the list? It isn't — keep reading.)

Compare your API's own token: "alg": "HS256", an HMAC with one shared secret that both signs and checks. That's right for a token only your backend ever makes and checks. It would be useless for Google: to let you check its tokens, Google would have to give you the secret — and then you could make them too.

You just read a token you can't trust

The sample above was signed by a key the TAs made, not by Google. Everything you decoded — iss: "https://accounts.google.com", email_verified: true — is just text someone typed. Decoding showed you what the token claims; it checked nothing. Telling a real Google token from this one takes the signature check against Google's keys — and its exp passed long ago, too. That's section 05.

Q1The payload says "email_verified": true. Can your React app believe it?
Show answer

Not on its own. The browser can decode any string that looks like a JWT, including one somebody wrote by hand, and has no business deciding who is logged in anyway. Only a verifier that checks the signature against Google's keys — your backend — can say "Google really said this".

Q2Why does Google sign with RS256 rather than HS256?
Show answer

Because millions of apps must be able to check Google's tokens without being able to make them. With a key pair, the public half checks and only the private half signs. With HS256 the same secret does both, so sharing it would let every app forge tokens.

05

Who checks what — and why your backend issues its own token

Corethe browser carries, the backend verifies

The browser must not decide

Everything in the browser is under the user's control: they can edit your JavaScript in DevTools, or skip your app and send requests with fetch by hand — you did, in React Lab 3 · §09. If the backend accepted "the frontend says this is asha.rao@example.com", anyone could be anyone. So the split is strict:

  • The browser gets the ID token from Google and carries it to your backend. It doesn't verify it, and doesn't need to.
  • The backend verifies it completely, then decides who this is.

Four checks, four attacks

"Verify completely" isn't one check; it's several, and each one stops a different attack. Skip any one and that attack works.

The attackThe check that stops it
Someone types up a payload — "email": "admin@esd.local" — base64url-encodes it and adds any signature. Or edits the email in a real token.Signature: RS256, checked with the Google key whose kid the header names. Only Google's private key makes a signature that matches.
They sign it themselves: their own RSA key, or "alg": "HS256" with a made-up secret, or "alg": "none" and no signature at all.Only RS256, only Google's keys. The algorithm and the keys come from your verifier's settings, never from the token's own header.
A genuine Google ID token — issued when the user signed in to some other website with Google, a quiz app, a game — is sent to your backend. That site's owner, holding tokens from all its users, can try each one on you.Audience: aud must be your client ID. Every site with a Google button receives ID tokens; this check is the only thing that stops all of them from logging in to your app as their users.
A token signed with someone else's real key — another identity provider, also using RS256.Issuer: iss is accounts.google.com or https://accounts.google.com — and the keys are fetched from Google's own URL.
A real token for your app, copied from a log, a screenshot, a shared computer — replayed tomorrow.Expiry: exp is one hour after issue. (Within that hour it does work again — section 15 shows it. Never log tokens.)

One more, after those four: email_verified must be true — an email Google hasn't verified proves nothing about who owns it.

Why the backend issues its own token

Once the backend believes the ID token, it could just tell the browser "fine, keep sending me Google's token". It doesn't. It finds or creates the user and answers with its own JWT — the same answer as POST /api/auth/login. Four reasons:

  1. One auth system for the rest of the app. Every protected endpoint already checks your token: the filter, the roles, /api/users/me. None of them change. A Google user is just a user.
  2. Roles live in your database, not at Google. Google knows Asha's email. It doesn't know she's a STUDENT, or that admin@esd.local is an ADMIN. Your token carries your answer.
  3. Lifetime and logout are yours. Your token's expiry, your deny-list on POST /api/auth/logout. Google's ID token is a one-time proof at the door, not a session.
  4. No call to Google on every request. After the exchange, Google isn't involved again until the next sign-in.

So the browser uses Google's token once, in one request, and drops it. From then on it holds your token, in AuthContext, like any password login.

Q1A classmate's backend checks the signature and the expiry, but not aud. Sign-in works perfectly in the browser. What's wrong?
Show answer

It accepts any genuine Google ID token, whichever app it was issued for. Anyone running a site with a Google button collects valid tokens for their visitors — and can sign in to your classmate's app as any of them. Nothing you'd see in normal use reveals it, which is why the audit reads the code instead of trusting a working demo. (The conformance suite can't catch it either — section 12.)

Google Identity Services in the browser

Corescript, initialize, renderButton, callback · src/auth/loadGoogle.js

Google Identity Services (GIS) is Google's JavaScript library for "Sign in with Google". It isn't an npm package: you load it from Google, https://accounts.google.com/gsi/client, and it puts a google object on window. You use three of its functions:

// 1. Tell GIS which app this is, and what to call when someone signs in. google.accounts.id.initialize({ client_id: "123456789012-abc123def456.apps.googleusercontent.com", callback: (response) => { response.credential // the ID token — a JWT string, as in section 04 response.select_by // how they chose: "btn", "user", "auto"… }, }); // 2. Draw the "Sign in with Google" button inside an element of your page. google.accounts.id.renderButton(element, { theme: "outline", size: "large", text: "signin_with" }); // 3. Optional: One Tap, the "Continue as Asha" prompt (section 10, stretch). google.accounts.id.prompt();

When someone clicks the button, Google opens its own window — a popup, or in some browsers a built-in dialog — on accounts.google.com. The password, the account chooser, two-factor: all on Google's page. Your page never sees any of it. When it's done, GIS calls your callback with the credential. That's all the browser gets, and all it needs.

Google's reference says to call initialize once, and that if you call it again, only the last call counts. Keep that in mind for section 08.

The client ID is public. There is no client secret.

The client ID is in your JavaScript, which every visitor downloads. It's meant to be: it names your app to Google, it doesn't prove anything. Anyone can put your client ID in their page — and Google will refuse to work there (next paragraph). The Google console also shows a client secret for every client. This flow doesn't use it, and a browser app could never keep a secret anyway: anything in .env that starts with VITE_ ends up in the JavaScript. If a tutorial or an AI asks you to put a client secret in your React app, it's describing a different flow.

No redirect URI — the origin is checked instead

Some OAuth flows end with Google sending the browser back to a URL on your server — a redirect URI — carrying a code the server swaps for tokens (the "authorization code flow"; Spring's oauth2Login() works that way, with sessions and cookies). The button flow doesn't: the token comes back to a JavaScript callback, on the page that asked. So instead of a redirect URI, Google checks the page's origin — scheme, host and port — against the client's Authorized JavaScript origins. For this course those are:

http://localhost http://localhost:5173

An origin is exact. http://127.0.0.1:5173 is a different origin (another host name), http://localhost:5174 is a different origin (another port), and Google refuses both with origin_mismatch. One more line in index.html is there for the same check: <meta name="referrer" content="no-referrer-when-downgrade">, which Google's setup guide asks for when testing on http://localhost. Leave it.

Loading the script — given

src/auth/loadGoogle.js adds Google's <script> to the page the first time it's asked, and returns a promise for window.google:

let loading = null; // the promise, while the script is loading export function loadGoogle() { if (window.google?.accounts?.id) return Promise.resolve(window.google); // already here if (!loading) { loading = new Promise((resolve, reject) => { const script = document.createElement("script"); script.src = "https://accounts.google.com/gsi/client"; script.async = true; script.onload = () => { loading = null; resolve(window.google); }; script.onerror = () => { loading = null; script.remove(); reject(new Error("Couldn't load Google sign-in…")); }; document.head.append(script); }); } return loading; }

(The real file also checks that window.google really appeared.) Two things to notice. It's asynchronous: when your component first renders, Google's code usually isn't there yet, so the component has to wait for it. And everyone asking at the same time gets the same promise, so however many times an effect runs, there's one <script> tag. It rejects when the script can't load — offline, or a privacy extension blocking accounts.google.com.

useRef — a box React doesn't watch

Corethe one new hook in this lab

Refresher

A component is a function React calls on every render; its local variables start fresh each time, and only state survives — and changing state re-renders (React Lab 1 · §07). A function created in one render and called later still sees that render's values: a closure, and when it's an old one, a stale closure (JS Lab 1 · §06).

Why it's earned now

Until now, React drew everything on the screen, from your state. You never needed the actual DOM element behind a <div>. GIS changes that: renderButton(element, …) wants a real DOM element to draw its button into — the button is Google's, an iframe React knows nothing about. In JS Lab 2 you'd have written document.getElementById. In React you don't: React decides when elements exist (not yet, during the first render), an id clashes if the component appears twice, and React may swap the element out. React's way to hand you its element is a ref.

What useRef gives you

const box = useRef(initialValue); // → { current: initialValue }

An object with one property, current, and two promises: it's the same object on every render (so whatever you put in current survives), and changing current doesn't re-render — React doesn't watch it. That makes it the third kind of place a value can live:

Where the value livesSurvives a re-render?Changing it re-renders?Use it for
let x inside the componentNo — new every renderNoValues worked out during render
useStateYesYesAnything the screen shows
useRefYesNoDOM elements; things the screen doesn't show

Job 1 — holding a DOM element

Pass the ref to an element's ref attribute, and React fills in current for you: after the element is on the page, current is that element; when it's removed, null. So you read it in effects and event handlers — which run after the page is updated — and never during render, when on the first render it's still null.

function SearchBox() { const inputRef = useRef(null); useEffect(() => { inputRef.current.focus(); // after render: the <input> exists }, []); return <input ref={inputRef} aria-label="Search" />; }

That's the button's case exactly: <div ref={buttonRef} />, and in an effect, renderButton(buttonRef.current, …).

Job 2 — a value that outlives a render, without being shown

GIS keeps the callback you give initialize() and calls it later — maybe many renders later, when someone finally clicks. The login page passes the button a handler, onCredential, and makes a new one on every render (every keystroke in the password field re-renders it). Hand GIS the handler from the first render, and later it calls that one: a stale closure. Re-run initialize on every render instead, and the button is torn down and redrawn on every keystroke. The way out is a box:

const onCredentialRef = useRef(onCredential); useEffect(() => { onCredentialRef.current = onCredential; // after every render: the latest handler }); // …and GIS is given a tiny function that looks in the box WHEN it's called: callback: (response) => onCredentialRef.current(response.credential),

GIS keeps the tiny function forever; the tiny function always finds the latest handler. This part is given in GoogleSignInButton.jsx — read it, and know why it's there. (Writing to current inside an effect, not during render, is React's rule: render should only calculate.)

What's still not yet: useMemo, useCallback and useReducer. (useCallback is another way to keep a handler from changing every render — but it would make every parent of the button responsible for it. The box keeps the button correct whatever its parent does.)

Q1Why not keep the button's element in useState?
Show answer

Nothing on the screen depends on it, so re-rendering when it changes would be wasted work — and you'd have to get it into state somehow, which is what a ref already does. State is for what the screen shows; a ref is for things React needs to keep but not draw from.

Q2You write console.log(buttonRef.current) in the component body, before the return. What does the first render print? And the second?
Show answer

null, then the <div>. During the first render the element doesn't exist yet — React creates it after your function returns. That's why the button is drawn in an effect, which runs after the page is updated.

3 · The verifier — the heart of it

import org.springframework.security.oauth2.core.*; // OAuth2TokenValidator, …Result, OAuth2Error, DelegatingOAuth2TokenValidator import org.springframework.security.oauth2.jwt.*; // Jwt, JwtDecoder, NimbusJwtDecoder, JwtTimestampValidator, JwtException @Component public class GoogleTokenVerifier { private static final String GOOGLE_KEYS = "https://www.googleapis.com/oauth2/v3/certs"; private static final Set<String> GOOGLE_ISSUERS = Set.of("accounts.google.com", "https://accounts.google.com"); private final JwtDecoder decoder; // a private field — NOT a @Bean (see below) public GoogleTokenVerifier(@Value("${app.google.client-id}") String clientId) { NimbusJwtDecoder decoder = NimbusJwtDecoder.withJwkSetUri(GOOGLE_KEYS).build(); // RS256, Google's keys OAuth2TokenValidator<Jwt> issuer = jwt -> GOOGLE_ISSUERS.contains(jwt.getClaimAsString("iss")) ? ok() : invalid("not issued by Google"); OAuth2TokenValidator<Jwt> audience = jwt -> jwt.getAudience() != null && jwt.getAudience().contains(clientId) ? ok() : invalid("issued for another app"); // setJwtValidator REPLACES the default checks — so the expiry check is put back by hand. decoder.setJwtValidator(new DelegatingOAuth2TokenValidator<Jwt>(new JwtTimestampValidator(), issuer, audience)); this.decoder = decoder; } public record GoogleProfile(String email, String name) {} public GoogleProfile verify(String idToken) { Jwt jwt; try { jwt = decoder.decode(idToken); // signature first, then exp, iss, aud — or a JwtException } catch (JwtException e) { throw new ApiException(HttpStatus.UNAUTHORIZED, "Invalid Google token"); } String email = jwt.getClaimAsString("email"); if (email == null || !Boolean.TRUE.equals(jwt.getClaimAsBoolean("email_verified"))) { throw new ApiException(HttpStatus.UNAUTHORIZED, "The Google account has no verified email"); } String name = jwt.getClaimAsString("name"); return new GoogleProfile(email.trim().toLowerCase(Locale.ROOT), name == null || name.isBlank() ? email : name); } private static OAuth2TokenValidatorResult ok() { return OAuth2TokenValidatorResult.success(); } private static OAuth2TokenValidatorResult invalid(String why) { return OAuth2TokenValidatorResult.failure(new OAuth2Error("invalid_token", why, null)); } }
LineWhich check, and why it's written this way
withJwkSetUri(GOOGLE_KEYS)Signature. Fetches Google's key set, picks the key named by the token's kid, and checks the RS256 signature. It accepts RS256 only, so none and HS256 tokens fail here. It caches the keys, and fetches again when it meets a kid it doesn't know (Google rotates its keys).
new JwtTimestampValidator()Expiry (and nbf), allowing 60 seconds of clock difference. A decoder checks this by default — but setJwtValidator replaces the defaults, so leave it out of the list and expired tokens are accepted.
issuerIssuer, both spellings. Spring's own JwtIssuerValidator takes one string, so it would refuse Google's other spelling.
audienceAudience: this app's client ID, from the property. The check that stops other apps' tokens (section 05).
catch (JwtException e)Any failure — bad signature, expired, wrong iss or aud, not a JWT at all — becomes your 401 ApiError. Not a 500, and no reason given to the caller (the log can have it).
email_verifiedAn email Google hasn't verified proves nothing.

Why not a @Bean? If your security setup checks your own tokens with Spring's resource server (as the reference backend does), Spring looks for the JwtDecoder bean. Declare Google's decoder as a second @Bean JwtDecoder and the app won't start ("expected single matching bean but found 2") — or, if it's the only one, Spring starts checking every request's token as if it were Google's, and all your own tokens get 401. As a private field inside the verifier, it can't be mistaken for anything.

Your backend now talks to Google. At the first sign-in it fetches www.googleapis.com/oauth2/v3/certs. On a network that blocks it, every Google sign-in fails with 401 — the log says the key set couldn't be retrieved.
